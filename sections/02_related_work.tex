\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related-work}

This section positions \sepa{} within four connected areas: synthesis of mixed-type health tables, evaluation beyond aggregate resemblance, rare-outcome learning under imbalance and overlap, and the privacy--utility tension. Each area supplies part of the required evaluation, but their intersection remains underexplored.

\subsection{Synthetic Tabular Health Data}

Tabular health data represent one person or encounter per row and heterogeneous clinical or survey variables in columns; synthetic microdata are artificial row-level records intended to reproduce selected properties of such data without directly reproducing the source rows. Statistical microdata synthesis predates deep learning. Multiple imputation and probabilistic graphical models generate records from estimated conditional distributions and remain attractive because their assumptions can be inspected \citep{raghunathan2003}. Copulas provide efficient dependence modelling but can struggle when discrete, continuous, multimodal, and rare-event variables interact nonlinearly \citep{wang2021}.

Generative adversarial networks formulate generation as an adversarial game \citep{goodfellow2014}. MedGAN adapted this approach to high-dimensional discrete patient records \citep{choi2017}, and CTGAN addressed mixed-type tables through mode-specific normalization, conditional vectors, and training-by-sampling \citep{xu2019}. Variational autoencoders optimize a likelihood-related lower bound \citep{kingma2014}; TVAE is a tabular extension evaluated alongside CTGAN \citep{xu2019}. TabDDPM later applied separate diffusion processes to numerical and categorical attributes and outperformed several GAN and VAE comparators on general tabular benchmarks \citep{kotelnikov2023}. These methods improve generation, whereas \sepa{} operates after generation: it tests whether transparent control of the candidate pool's class geometry can improve rare-outcome utility. We use CTGAN and TVAE to examine transfer across two generator families; diffusion remains a future comparator.

\subsection{Evaluation Beyond Aggregate Resemblance}

Health-synthesis studies repeatedly conclude that no single metric establishes usefulness or safety and that evaluation should jointly cover resemblance, downstream utility, and privacy \citep{goncalves2020,hernandez2023,pezoulas2024}. Recent comparisons report dataset- and metric-dependent rankings and a persistent fidelity/utility--privacy trade-off \citep{hernandez2025,nanevski2026}. A review of structured synthetic electronic health records also documents continuing problems with rare events, reproducibility, and privacy evaluation \citep{ghosheh2024}.

Univariate fidelity measures whether synthetic columns resemble their real counterparts, pairwise fidelity evaluates dependence structure, and multivariate distinguishability tests whether a classifier separates real from synthetic records \citep{goncalves2020,hernandez2023}. None alone establishes rare-outcome usefulness: aggregate scores can be dominated by negative-class records, whereas a selector can improve classification by producing easy positive prototypes rather than a faithful positive population \citep{ghosheh2024,pezoulas2024}.

Train-on-Synthetic, Test-on-Real (\tstr{}) evaluation probes whether relations learned from synthetic records transfer to held-out real observations; Real-plus-Synthetic-to-Real evaluation instead tests augmentation \citep{goncalves2020,hernandez2023}. These regimes answer different questions and are reported separately here. Neither constitutes a privacy guarantee or proves unbiased clinical inference.

\subsection{Class Imbalance, Overlap, and Augmentation}

Accuracy can remain high while positive-class recall is poor in imbalanced data \citep{he2009}. Macro-F1 gives both classes equal weight, while AUPRC is often more informative than AUROC for rare positive outcomes \citep{saito2015}. Conventional approaches include random resampling, SMOTE \citep{chawla2002}, cost-sensitive learning, and threshold adjustment \citep{he2009}. These methods alter frequency or decision cost but do not necessarily recover informative class-conditional structure.

A recent comparison across 13 small health datasets found that sampling with replacement could match more complex generative augmentation in several settings, suggesting that some apparent gains arise mainly from increased sample size \citep{huetdastarac2026}. This makes random oversampling a necessary comparator and sharpens our question: does controlling generated class geometry add value beyond correcting sample counts?

Class imbalance and class overlap are related but distinct \citep{he2009}. A rare positive class can remain geometrically well separated, whereas a less imbalanced outcome can be intrinsically difficult because valid observations occupy overlapping feature regions. Adding examples cannot remove irreducible overlap, and aggressively selecting distant examples may improve an empirical boundary by deleting difficult but legitimate cases. We therefore interpret discrimination jointly with calibration and positive-class coverage and diversity.

\subsection{Privacy--Utility Tension}

Synthetic data are not anonymous by construction: high-capacity generators can reproduce or reveal information about training records \citep{yale2019,kaabachi2025}. Privacy can be examined through nearest-record, membership-inference, and attribute-inference analyses or protected through formal mechanisms such as differential privacy \citep{dwork2006}. LDP-GAN combines local differential privacy with medical-record synthesis \citep{kim2024ldpgan}, while a recent oncology framework evaluates candidate synthetic datasets across multiple quality dimensions before downstream use \citep{christoforou2025}. Accordingly, our study reports empirical privacy proxies separately from fidelity and utility and does not equate a near-chance proxy with anonymity.

\subsection{Research Gap and Study Positioning}

Table~\ref{tab:related-comparison} summarizes how representative studies address the dimensions central to this work. The literature leaves a practical gap at the intersection of rare outcomes, explicit class-overlap control, held-out-real augmentation, positive-class coverage, and privacy diagnostics. Generators optimize distribution learning, imbalance methods correct representation or decision cost, and evaluation frameworks generally assess outputs after generation rather than steering class-conditional geometry. Our contribution is not a universally superior generator; it is an auditable intervention and evaluation design for determining when separability-aware selection helps and what it may sacrifice.
