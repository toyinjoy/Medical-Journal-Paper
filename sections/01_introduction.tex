\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Synthetic tabular data are increasingly used when health records are sensitive, access is restricted, or the outcome of interest has relatively few positive observations. The intended benefit is not simply to create more rows, but to preserve enough class-conditional information for a model trained on synthetic records to perform on real patients or survey respondents. Generation alone does not guarantee that property, nor does it guarantee privacy \citep{goncalves2020,hernandez2023,kaabachi2025}. A generator can reproduce aggregate distributions while attenuating the decision structure of a minority outcome, omitting rare positive modes, or creating records that are easy to classify but unrepresentative of the positive population \citep{ghosheh2024,pezoulas2024}.

Class imbalance and class overlap are related but different problems. Random oversampling, undersampling, SMOTE, class weighting, and conditional generation change the number or influence of positive observations \citep{chawla2002,he2009}. They do not necessarily determine which synthetic observations carry useful boundary information. Conversely, selecting only highly classifiable synthetic cases may improve minority detection by concentrating prototypes while removing difficult but legitimate cases. A useful synthetic-data intervention therefore has to be evaluated along at least three axes: predictive utility on untouched real data, coverage of the positive-class support, and calibration of the resulting predictions.

Most synthetic-health-data frameworks assess a completed synthetic dataset through fidelity, downstream utility, and privacy diagnostics \citep{goncalves2020,hernandez2023,hernandez2025}. Less attention has been paid to the intermediate curation decision: given one fixed generator and an oversized candidate pool, which records should be retained, when does that choice change downstream utility, and what is lost through the choice? This gap is practically important because a post-generation selector cannot create a mode that the generator failed to produce, but it can alter how available modes are represented.

We address this problem with \sepa{}, a transparent post-generation curation method. The present paper fixes CTGAN \citep{xu2019} as the generator and does not compare generator families. CTGAN was chosen before the locked evaluation because an earlier development study in this research program found it to provide the strongest and most stable candidate pools for the selection hypothesis. That development result motivates the generator choice but is not reused as confirmatory evidence here.

The study is organized by a Generate--Curate--Decide framework. \emph{Generate} asks whether the oversized CTGAN pool contains useful class-conditional modes. \emph{Curate} applies Pareto ordering over realism, separability, and prespecified profile compatibility, then repairs diversity when the selected set becomes too concentrated. \emph{Decide} trains a classifier on the selected synthetic data and learns its operating threshold without access to real validation or test labels. This decomposition distinguishes generator adequacy, selection concentration, and synthetic-to-real probability shift.

\subsection{Research questions}

\begin{description}[leftmargin=1.2cm,style=nextline]
\item[\textbf{RQ1:}] Does separability-aware curation improve locked held-out-real minority-class F1 relative to quota-matched standard CTGAN sampling when the generator, candidate pool, class counts, classifiers, and threshold policy are held constant?
\item[\textbf{RQ2:}] Are any gains accompanied by changes in Macro-F1, ranking utility, calibration, positive-class coverage, or diversity?
\item[\textbf{RQ3:}] Which candidate-pool and dataset conditions distinguish successful curation from unstable or unhelpful selection?
\end{description}

The contributions are fourfold. First, we define an auditable curation operator that separates row selection from generator fitting and class counts. Second, we evaluate a frozen candidate and inference plan on an internal locked partition across four health outcome tasks. Third, a progressive replacement intervention isolates the effect of selected rows without regenerating the candidate pool. Fourth, we report the operating limits with the positive findings: calibration deterioration, support narrowing, training-budget sensitivity, and the failure of selection to recover modes absent from a pool. The goal is not to present a universally superior synthetic-data method, but to identify when curation is useful and when its apparent utility should not be trusted.

The remainder of the paper positions this question within synthetic health data and imbalanced learning, defines the Generate--Curate--Decide protocol, reports locked and mechanism results, and discusses the resulting applicability envelope.
