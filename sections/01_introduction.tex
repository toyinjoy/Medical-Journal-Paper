\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Health datasets combine sensitive information, mixed numerical and categorical variables, missingness, and outcomes that are often uncommon but clinically consequential. Synthetic tabular data can support model development and collaboration when access to real records is restricted, but generation alone does not guarantee privacy or downstream usefulness \citep{goncalves2020,hernandez2023,kaabachi2025}. In particular, a generator may preserve aggregate distributions while weakening the class-conditional structure needed to identify a rare outcome or may yield deceptively easy synthetic examples \citep{ghosheh2024,pezoulas2024}.

Rare-outcome learning is commonly framed as a class-imbalance problem. Random resampling, the Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique (SMOTE), class weighting, and conditional generation increase the number or influence of positive-class observations \citep{chawla2002,he2009}. More data, however, need not provide more information when positive and negative observations overlap in feature space. Class prevalence and class overlap are distinct properties: data-level or algorithm-level imbalance correction changes representation or decision cost, whereas it does not necessarily repair weak class-conditional geometry \citep{he2009}.

Existing synthetic-health-data evaluations typically assess global resemblance, downstream utility, and privacy after generation \citep{goncalves2020,hernandez2023,pezoulas2024}. They provide less guidance on whether post-generation control of class geometry improves rare-outcome prediction, when such control fails, and whether apparent gains arise by discarding difficult but legitimate positive cases. This gap motivates the present study. Our objective is to determine whether separability-aware candidate selection adds predictive value beyond class-count correction and standard generation, while explicitly measuring calibration, positive-class coverage and diversity, and empirical privacy risk.

We developed \sepa{}, a post-generation mechanism that draws an oversized candidate pool and ranks records using proximity to the real training manifold and label-consistent geometric evidence. It retains a prespecified class count, separating the effect of candidate selection from a change in class ratio. We first isolate the mechanism in Vigitel obesity and COVID-19 hospital mortality, then evaluate practical augmentation across those tasks plus Vigitel current smoking and landmark-defined kidney-cohort mortality.

The principal result is deliberately conditional. Within CTGAN candidate pools, \sepa{} increased mean AUPRC relative to standard CTGAN by 0.015 for COVID-19 mortality, 0.013 for Vigitel obesity, and 0.037 for Vigitel smoking, but by only 0.0003 for kidney mortality. Real-only training or simple resampling remained competitive or superior on several endpoints, TVAE results were inconsistent, and improved positive-class coverage repeatedly coincided with reduced diversity. Thus separability control can help in some settings, but it is neither a universal substitute for imbalance correction nor evidence that selected records preserve all clinically meaningful heterogeneity.

\subsection{Research Questions}

The study addresses three research questions:
\begin{description}[leftmargin=1.2cm,style=nextline]
\item[\textbf{RQ1:}] Is data-level or algorithm-level class-imbalance correction sufficient, or does class overlap limit the utility of synthetic augmentation?
\item[\textbf{RQ2:}] Does separability-aware selection improve rare-outcome prediction beyond random sampling, SMOTE, class weighting, and unconstrained generation?
\item[\textbf{RQ3:}] When predictive performance improves, is the gain accompanied by acceptable calibration, positive-class coverage and diversity, and empirical privacy risk?
\end{description}

The mechanism experiment uses leakage-controlled synthetic-to-real evaluation followed by a blocked $2\times2$ factorial analysis that separates separability weighting from clinical-anchor weighting. The strengthened experiment evaluates real-plus-synthetic augmentation on shared held-out real folds using real-only training, conventional imbalance methods, CTGAN, TVAE, and \sepa{} variants. AUPRC is the primary augmentation endpoint; secondary analyses include Macro-F1, positive-class metrics, AUROC, balanced accuracy, Brier score, calibration, coverage, diversity, and privacy-risk proxies.

\subsection{Contributions}

This four-task study makes four contributions:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item It frames class overlap as a target distinct from class imbalance and global fidelity in synthetic health-data augmentation.
\item It introduces an auditable post-generation selection mechanism whose effect can be separated from generator fitting and class-count correction.
\item It uses a factorial experiment to attribute the strong Vigitel obesity synthetic-to-real change to separability weighting while reporting the COVID-19 null attribution with equal emphasis.
\item It tests the easier-case explanation through a strengthened benchmark spanning four tasks, two generator families, conventional imbalance baselines, calibration, positive-class structure, and privacy diagnostics.
\end{enumerate}

The remainder of the paper reviews synthetic tabular health data and rare-outcome evaluation, defines the datasets and leakage-controlled protocols, reports the mechanism and four-task augmentation results, interprets their boundary conditions, and concludes with limitations and requirements for external validation.
